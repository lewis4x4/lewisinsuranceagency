# Florida Raised Its Crash-Reporting Threshold to $2,000 on Oct. 1, 2026: What Drivers Should Know

A Florida traffic law that changed on **October 1, 2026** affects what you do in the first few minutes after a fender-bender. The Legislature raised the property damage amount that requires a driver to **immediately notify law enforcement** of a crash. It went from **$500 to $2,000**.

Here's what the new rule says, what it doesn't change, and how it fits with your auto insurance claim.

## What changed

Section 316.065(1), Florida Statutes, tells drivers when they must report a crash to police right away. Senate Bill 488 became **Chapter 2026-39, Laws of Florida**. Section 7 of that law changed the trigger to apparent damage to any vehicle or other property of **at least $2,000**. Before, the trigger was $500. The law took effect **October 1, 2026**.

The updated statute now says a driver must "immediately by the quickest means of communication" notify law enforcement after any crash that causes:

- **injury to or death of any person**, or
- **damage to any vehicle or other property in an apparent amount of at least $2,000.**

## What did not change

- **Any injury still means you call.** The $2,000 figure applies only to property damage. If anyone is hurt, the reporting duty applies no matter how small the damage looks.
- **You still have to exchange information.** Under s. 316.062, drivers in a crash that damages a vehicle or property must give their name, address, and vehicle registration number. If asked, they must also show their driver's license. Drivers must also give reasonable help to anyone who is injured.
- **Who you call stays the same.** Inside city limits, call the local police department. Outside a city, call the county sheriff's office or the nearest Florida Highway Patrol station. In our area, that means Lake City Police inside the city limits. In unincorporated Columbia County, call the Columbia County Sheriff's Office or FHP.
- **Breaking the rule is still a traffic infraction.** Failing to report when the law requires it is a noncriminal, nonmoving traffic infraction under chapter 318.

## "Apparent" damage is a judgment call

The statute uses the words "apparent amount." That means your best estimate at the scene, before anyone writes a repair estimate. Modern bumpers, headlights, and sensor-equipped parts can cost more to fix than they look. If you aren't sure whether a crash crosses $2,000, the safer choice is to call.

## How this affects your auto insurance claim

The new threshold sets when the **law** requires a police report. It doesn't change what your **insurance policy** asks of you. Most auto policies require prompt notice of an accident, even a minor one. Check your policy for its exact wording.

Even when police notice isn't required, protect your claim:

1. **Get the other driver's details:** name, address, plate, registration, and insurance information.
2. **Take photos** of both vehicles, the scene, and any road conditions.
3. **Write down** the time, location, and any witness names and phone numbers.
4. **Contact your insurer or agent promptly**, even if you think the damage is minor.

A police report isn't legally required below the new threshold. Still, it can help document what happened if fault is disputed later. You can still call for one.
